<p>This particular image from my blog post a couple days ago has resulted in many questions. Here’s a little more information to put this photo in some kind of historical perspective…</p>

<p>This was the last train ever to come through Chateaugay. This was called the "scrapper" train. It was taking up the rails as the Rutland Railway completely closed down its northern New York line.</p>

<p>The photographer was standing in front of the depot (probably near where the Chateaugay Historical Society has placed the historic marker) and facing west. This was long before the old Whistle Stop was built. Then, the lawn extended from the depot building all the way to the sidewalk on Depot Street. Just to the left of the engine is the corner of the old Spellman building (that Barry Barnes tore down to build his garage that Rodney Davis now owns). </p>

<p>In the center of the photo, just at the other end of the engine, are the roof lines of some of the houses on Iron Avenue. The darker shaded building (behind the RR stop signal) and the other building corner just on the right side of the photo were freight/storage buildings that were torn down shortly after the RR was closed. </p>

<p>You can also see a man in a dark suit about to cross Depot Street. He's walking parallel to the actual tracks. There are a bunch of kids on their bikes on the west side of Depot Street, south of the tracks. I'm sure they all pedaled down to watch that last engine come through the village. There was a small crowd of people who came to the tracks to see this last engine pass through that day. </p>

<p>The RR ran through Chateaugay for 115 years and 3 days! The first train rolled through on June 1, 1850. Nearly everyone in the community came to Depot Street that day to see that first steam engine chug through, whistle sounding and steam billowing from its stack. </p>

<p>The railroad was finally able to open because of the completion of the earthen trestle across the Chateaugay River, just west of the village. Finishing that huge project delayed the opening of the entire line. Trains ran back and forth on the east (Rouses Point to Chateaugay) and west (Ogdensburg to Burke) portions of the tracks, but the entire line was not opened until that trestle was completed. Finally, a train could begin at Rouses Point and go all the way to Ogdensburg, and back again without any delay or hindrance.</p>

<p>That earthen trestle, by the way, was all completed with hand labor. All the estimated 400,000 cubic yards of fill were dug with pick and shovel, loaded onto horse-drawn wagons, transported to the embankment, and unloaded, shovelful by shovelful, to bring the embankment up to the level needed to join the east and west portions of already laid tracks. </p>

<p>This all took place after crews had already completed the stone tunnel that funneled the Chateaugay River through this huge earthwork project.</p>

<p>The chief engineer for the Northern Railroad construction, Charles L. Schlatter, estimated that the fill required to form the embankment was the equivalent of taking a 16-acre farm and removing all the soil from it to a depth of 16 feet! Imagine thinking of that task using only a small army of men armed with only picks and shovels! A monumental task, to say the least. No wonder it held up the official opening of the entire rail line.</p>

<p>Unfortunately, the crowd that gathered to see this last train come through paled in comparison to the huge crowd that had gathered to see the first engine pass through. </p>

<p>Back in 1850, that first locomotive represented the great promise and potential that the rails would bring to Chateaugay. The local population was the highest (3728 people according to the 1850 US Census) it would ever be and local dairy farmers, livestock operations, and crop farmers (think potatoes and hops, for example) were ready to increase their production as the Northern Railroad meant that their products could now be shipped far and wide.</p>

<p>All things change and evolve, of course. The local railroad operation came and went. It was wildly successful in its heyday, but its profitability fell and the inevitable end came. </p>

<p>This wonderful photo, taken on Friday, June 4, 1965, shows the scene of that entire local railroad experience coming to an end, 115 years and 3 days after that first train rumbled through town.</p>
